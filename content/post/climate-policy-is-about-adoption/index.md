Climate policy is often narrated as a race to invent better technology. We need cheaper solar panels, denser batteries, longer-range electric vehicles, and more efficient heat pumps.

Innovation is essential. But many climate technologies already work. The harder problem is increasingly adoption.

Who installs them? Who does not? Who makes the decision, who pays, and who receives the benefits? What happens when the technology is financially attractive but still peripheral to the person responsible for implementing it?

Our research on California's rooftop solar mandates made those questions concrete.

## Technical potential is not social implementation

An engineering model can estimate how much electricity a solar system will produce. It can combine local sunlight, roof area, electricity use, equipment costs, financing, and incentives. Under plausible assumptions, rooftop solar had positive expected private returns for nearly all projects in our Bay Area sample.

Yet voluntary adoption remained incomplete, and larger modeled returns did little to explain which builders installed solar.

That gap illustrates a general principle: technical potential is not an outcome. Between the two lie markets, institutions, financing, administrative processes, and human attention.

## Prices are powerful, but they do not solve every friction

Economists often prefer price-based policies because they allow people and firms to find the cheapest way to reduce emissions. Carbon prices, tax credits, and rebates can be efficient and flexible.

But a price signal may be weak when the decision-maker does not capture the future benefit. A builder may not value the homeowner's electricity savings. A landlord may not value a tenant's lower utility bills. A homeowner may not finance a wildfire retrofit whose benefit is a lower probability of a distant loss.

In those settings, policy must engage with the institutional structure of the decision rather than merely raise the estimated return.

## Affordability is more than positive net present value

Calling an investment "profitable" can obscure the constraints households face. A positive lifetime payoff does not supply cash for the upfront cost. It does not guarantee access to credit. It does not reduce the complexity of comparing contractors or navigating permits. It does not make uncertain future savings as salient as today's purchase price.

That distinction becomes especially important when climate policy shifts costs onto housing. A rooftop solar mandate may produce valuable energy savings, but those savings arrive over time while construction costs are capitalized immediately. Understanding who can finance the transition and who captures its benefits is part of evaluating the policy, not an afterthought.

## Open models help expose assumptions

The System Advisor Model we used is public and extensively documented. That openness matters. Readers can examine assumptions about tariffs, system degradation, financing, and incentives. They can see why estimated payoffs change and where uncertainty enters.

Open data and models do not eliminate disagreement. They make the basis of disagreement visible. That is particularly important as private climate-risk and technology analytics increasingly influence decisions while keeping methods proprietary.

## From mitigation to adaptation

The adoption problem extends naturally from clean-energy technology to climate adaptation. Communities may know that wildfire, heat, or flood risks are increasing and still underinvest in protection. The reasons may include affordability, imperfect risk information, split incentives, uncertainty about public assistance, or the expectation that someone else will act.

Better hazard models are necessary, but not sufficient. Researchers also need to understand how households, insurers, builders, lenders, and governments use the information. A precise risk estimate has limited public value if it is inaccessible, unaffordable, or disconnected from feasible actions.

## What I take from the California experience

The California mandates increased solar adoption. That is an important result, but the broader lesson is about the path from evidence to implementation.

A technology can work. It can save money. Policymakers can publicize those benefits. Adoption can still remain far below technical potential.

Climate policy therefore needs both better technologies and better institutions for deploying them. The most useful research sits at that boundary: measuring what is possible, observing what actually happens, and identifying the policies that can close the distance between the two.
