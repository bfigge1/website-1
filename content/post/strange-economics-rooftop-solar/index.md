Most climate discussions begin with technology: how efficient are solar panels, how quickly are battery costs falling, or how far can an electric vehicle travel? Those are important questions. But the question that interests me more is why technologies that already appear economically attractive often spread so slowly.

Imagine two newly constructed homes in the same California subdivision. They have similar roofs, receive the same sunshine, and face the same electricity prices. An engineering model predicts that installing rooftop solar would save the future owner of either home money over the life of the system.

One home gets solar. The other does not.

Why?

That puzzle motivated our paper, [Solar Adoption by Mandates](/publication/solar_mandates/). California offered an unusually useful setting because it moved from voluntary adoption to mandatory solar requirements. San Francisco adopted a citywide requirement in 2017. California followed with a statewide requirement for most new low-rise residential construction in January 2020.

The policy shift let us ask two related but distinct questions. First, did mandates actually increase adoption? Second, why were builders not already installing solar on many projects where it appeared to pay?

## Solar can be profitable without being important

Using the National Renewable Energy Laboratory's System Advisor Model, we estimated the private financial payoff from rooftop solar for thousands of Bay Area construction projects. Under our primary assumptions, expected net present values were positive for every project in the sample. For single-family homes, estimated payoffs ranged from about $2,700 to $17,200, with a median near $7,900.

Those numbers sound substantial in isolation. But a new home is an expensive and complicated project. The median construction cost in the subset with cost data was about $500,000. For most projects, the expected solar payoff amounted to less than 2 percent of total project cost, and for almost all projects it was below 5 percent.

That scale matters. A builder managing financing, inspections, contractors, architecture, and a long construction schedule may pay little attention to a benefit that accrues mainly to the future occupant. A few thousand dollars of additional design, administrative, or interconnection costs could erase much of the estimated return. Solar may be profitable while still being peripheral to the builder's decision.

## The engineer's answer and the builder's answer

An engineering model asks whether a system can generate enough valuable electricity to justify its cost. It combines sunlight, roof space, hourly electricity use, utility rates, system degradation, financing, incentives, and installation costs.

A builder faces a different problem. Will buyers pay more for the home? Will solar complicate design or delay the project? Who receives the future electricity savings? Are those savings large enough to matter relative to the overall development?

The engineer's answer can therefore be "yes" while the builder's answer is "not worth the trouble."

This distinction is central to climate policy. Technologies do not diffuse simply because they work or because a spreadsheet reports a positive return. Adoption depends on institutions, split incentives, financing, information, transaction costs, and who is making the decision.

## What the mandates changed

Both mandates increased rooftop solar adoption. Our synthetic-control estimate indicates that San Francisco's policy raised the share of new residential buildings with solar by 33 percentage points, approximately doubling the pre-policy adoption rate. The statewide mandate also produced a clear increase in solar capacity installed per new residential permit.

But neither policy produced perfect measured compliance. San Francisco allowed exemptions and alternative compliance pathways. The statewide data also show post-policy adoption below what a literal 100-percent requirement might suggest. That is not merely a technical footnote: policy design, enforcement, exemptions, and measurement determine how a mandate works in practice.

## The broader lesson

I do not think rooftop solar is the only interesting part of this story. The same puzzle appears with heat pumps, insulation, batteries, electric vehicles, and wildfire retrofits. A technology can be technically feasible and privately beneficial while adoption remains incomplete.

Making clean technologies cheaper is essential. But the next generation of climate policy also needs to understand implementation: who decides, who pays, who benefits, and what frictions remain after the engineering problem is solved.
