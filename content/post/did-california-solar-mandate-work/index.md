California has adopted one of the most ambitious residential solar policies in the world. Beginning in January 2020, the state's building energy code required photovoltaic systems on most newly constructed low-rise residential buildings.

Did it work?

The answer is yes: both California's statewide policy and San Francisco's earlier citywide mandate substantially increased solar adoption. But the details complicate any simple story of automatic, universal compliance.

## Why before-and-after is not enough

Solar adoption was already changing before either mandate. Equipment costs were falling, federal incentives evolved, utility tariffs changed, and public interest increased. Comparing 2019 with 2021 would mix the mandate's effect with all of those trends. The start of the statewide policy also coincided almost immediately with the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted construction and permitting.

We used synthetic control methods to estimate a no-policy counterfactual. The method constructs a weighted combination of untreated places that closely tracks the treated jurisdiction before the policy. If the treated place then diverges after implementation, the gap provides an estimate of the policy effect.

## San Francisco: a 33-point increase

San Francisco's Better Roofs Ordinance took effect in 2017. Before implementation, about 27.5 percent of new residential buildings in the city adopted solar. A synthetic comparison built primarily from Oakland and San Jose closely tracked San Francisco's pre-policy pattern.

After implementation, the series diverged. Our main estimate is that the ordinance increased the solar share of new residential construction by **33 percentage points**, approximately a 120-percent increase relative to the pre-policy rate. A synthetic difference-in-differences specification produced a similar estimate of 25.4 percentage points.

Post-policy adoption was about 55 percent rather than 100 percent. That partly reflects the ordinance itself: San Francisco permitted exemptions and alternative compliance through solar thermal systems or green roofs. Permit records also do not perfectly reveal every compliance pathway.

## California: more capacity on each new building

For the statewide policy, consistent building-level adoption data were not available for all comparison jurisdictions. We therefore used photovoltaic capacity installed per new residential building permit. California's pre-policy level was already high, so the relevant question was whether capacity rose beyond the trajectory predicted by comparable places.

It did. Across synthetic-control and regression-based approaches, the statewide mandate increased installed photovoltaic capacity per new residential permit. The estimated increment was close to the capacity of a typical newly installed residential system, consistent with the mandate adding a rooftop array to many projects that otherwise would not have included one.

## Why measured adoption remained below 100 percent

A legal requirement does not necessarily translate into a dataset showing one installation for every permit. Several factors intervene:

- some buildings are exempt because of shading, roof configuration, or other technical constraints;
- projects may comply through community solar or other allowed pathways;
- permit issuance and solar interconnection occur at different points in a long construction timeline;
- datasets may not perfectly link every solar installation to its building permit; and
- enforcement and local implementation vary.

This is not a reason to dismiss the policy effect. It is a reason to distinguish statutory design, measured compliance, and causal impact.

## Was the mandate justified?

Our paper does not provide a complete welfare analysis. Rooftop solar produces social benefits and system costs that are outside the private-payoff model. Mandates can also be less flexible than market-based climate policies.

But the evidence supports two premises behind the policy. First, rooftop solar was privately cost-effective for many new buildings that nevertheless did not adopt it. Second, mandates caused a large increase in adoption.

Whether that increase is worth the costs depends on emissions benefits, grid effects, distributional consequences, enforcement costs, and the alternatives available to policymakers. What the California experience clearly shows is that regulation can move adoption far beyond the voluntary baseline - even for a mature technology with apparently positive private returns.
